<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-8 items-center">
  <div class="flex flex-col justify-center space-y-3">
    <p>Looking for something to do on a Friday night, I discovered Louisiana's Gift to America: A Musical Legacy happening at the <a href="https://shreveportmunicipalauditorium.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">Shreveport Municipal Auditorium</a>. The show featured three amazing Louisiana musical acts: <a href="https://compassrecords.com/artist/beausoleil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">BeauSoleil avec Michael Doucet</a>, the Dr. Michael White Quartet, and AJ Haynes of the <a href="https://www.seratones.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">Seratones</a>. The entire event made me so proud to be from the culturally rich state of Louisiana.</p>
    <p>The show followed the format of the famed <a href="https://shreveportmunicipalauditorium.com/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">Louisiana Hayride</a>, held on the same stage and broadcast by radio every Saturday night from April of 1948 until August of 1960. In between the musical sets, the artists were introduced with a fireside-style chat with host Ben Sandmel, a Louisiana author, folklorist, historian, and drummer.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="aspect-[3/4] w-full overflow-hidden rounded-xl border border-neutral-200 shadow-sm dark:border-neutral-800">
    <img src="/images/posts/2026-09-18-musical-legacy/musical-legacy-ben-sandmel.webp" alt="Host Ben Sandmel hosting fireside chats with featured Louisiana musicians" class="w-full h-full object-cover" />
  </div>
</div>

<div class="mb-8 space-y-3">
  <p>To warm up the crowd before the official program began, AJ Haynes kicked off the show with Lead Belly's version of "In the Pines." Her rendition of this song alone made me an instant fan. This was the first time I heard this young lady perform live, and what an amazing voice and range she has!</p>
  <p>The event introductions and welcome were made by Miranda Restovic of the <a href="https://leh.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">Louisiana Endowment for the Humanities</a>, acknowledging the program sponsors, while Leea Russell from the <a href="https://www.crt.state.la.us/cultural-development/arts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">Louisiana Division of the Arts</a> highlighted the purpose of the event in recognizing Louisiana's contribution to music across the nation. Ben Sandmel was formally introduced and hosted the evening with alternating brief interviews with the artists prior to their performance.</p>
</div>

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-8 items-center">
  <div class="aspect-[3/4] w-full overflow-hidden rounded-xl border border-neutral-200 shadow-sm dark:border-neutral-800">
    <img src="/images/posts/2026-09-18-musical-legacy/musical-legacy-aj-haynes.webp" alt="AJ Haynes performing live on acoustic guitar at Shreveport Municipal Auditorium" class="w-full h-full object-cover" />
  </div>
  <div class="flex flex-col justify-center space-y-3">
    <p class="font-bold text-lg">AJ Haynes</p>
    <p>The first talent featured was AJ Haynes, who discussed her upbringing in Columbia and various musical influences, including Lead Belly and Bob Dylan. She talked about putting on punk shows in her musical beginnings, and the connections between gospel, blues, rock 'n' roll, and punk. She expressed a love for exchanging ideas through music brought to Louisiana via the Transatlantic slave trade. She identifies the artist's role as a healer, where her interests include how to integrate the arts and wellness. She is currently an artist-in-residence at the <a href="https://www.ashenola.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline hover:opacity-80">Ashé Cultural Arts Center</a> in New Orleans.</p>
    <p>Her musical set included, from the Seratones' most recent album <em>Love & Algorhythms</em>, "Dark Matter"; a poem entitled "Evidence"; a song in Spanish that she wrote with a friend from Panama named "Milagrosa Creación" as an affirmation for migrant children to grow up without fear and blossom into miracles; and her final song, dedicated to our resilience and survival, called "For the Canaries."</p>
    <p class="italic font-medium">"She just strapped on that guitar and sang, honey."</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="mb-4">

</div>

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-8 items-center">
  <div class="flex flex-col justify-center space-y-3">
    <p>Next, Ben Sandmel called to the stage clarinetist Dr. Michael White. They discussed the evolution of music from Congo Square, the West African influence of dance and music, and specifically, the distinctive flavor of the New Orleans beat. Dr. White discussed his first social club parade in Marrero and the transition of jazz and brass-band music in New Orleans. He discussed how the funeral parades today are very different than the funeral parades of the 1970s. Dr. White discussed the differences today, not only in sound but also in dress. Playing with many musicians born in the 1890s, he absorbed traditional values of jazz music that aren't necessarily present in modern-day jazz. The discussion was informative and the performance was incredible.</p>
    <p>Dr. Michael White was accompanied by Tyler Thompson on bass, John Bradley on banjo, and on vocals and trumpet, Greg Stafford. Dr. Michael White's Quartet performed the march "Panama," the hymn "Sweet By and By," the blues original "Eternally Blue," and by Joe Darensbourg, "Louisiana, Eh Yeah" (aka "Eh La Bas").</p>
    <p class="italic font-medium">"Red Beans and Rice, yeah, I'm in paradise."</p>
  </div>

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-8 items-center">
  <div class="flex flex-col justify-center space-y-3">
    <p>The final artist brought on stage for a brief chat with Ben Sandmel was Cajun fiddler, Michael Doucet. The two discussed how the music was never called Cajun music but rather French music, chanky-chank, or old people music.</p>
    <p>Michael Doucet discussed being influenced by a large family where everyone played all kinds of instruments and sang. He was greatly influenced by a relative who was a singer and storyteller. After she died, it seemed the stories were just gone and he decided to research and reintroduce the music. After spending time in France and hearing the same ballads his aunts sang when he was a child, this really changed his perspective on the history and importance of this music.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="aspect-[3/4] w-full overflow-hidden rounded-xl border border-neutral-200 shadow-sm dark:border-neutral-800">
    <img src="/images/posts/2026-09-18-musical-legacy/musical-legacy-beausoleil-1.webp" alt="Close-up of Michael Doucet fiddling alongside accordionist Chad Huval" class="w-full h-full object-cover" />
  </div>
</div>

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-8 items-center">
  <div class="aspect-[3/4] w-full overflow-hidden rounded-xl border border-neutral-200 shadow-sm dark:border-neutral-800">
    <img src="/images/posts/2026-09-18-musical-legacy/musical-legacy-david-doucet.webp" alt="David Doucet performing on guitar with BeauSoleil" class="w-full h-full object-cover" />
  </div>
  <div class="flex flex-col justify-center space-y-3">
    <p>While taking a class at LSU about folk songs (with no mention of French music), he reviewed Irene Whitfield's 1939 thesis on French folk songs from Southwest Louisiana because up until this point, Cajun music was strictly passed orally from generation to generation. Eventually, he and others went out and interviewed the elder musicians who historically performed this style of music and got them back to performing in an effort to save this particular genre of music played in Louisiana. During the discussion, he specifically credited Louise Olivier and Irene Whitfield for inspiring the resurgence of interest in Cajun Music.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 mb-8 items-center">
  <div class="flex flex-col justify-center space-y-3">
    <p>When asked what it felt like to be the first Cajun band to win a Grammy Award, he remarked that BeauSoleil has been together for 52 years! In 1986 Beau Jocque had some hits and Cajun music began to gain popularity. There were lots of Cajun musicians featured on the show that year and they didn't expect to win as they were up against the likes of Bob Dylan and other well-known artists. When Whoopi Goldberg and Art Garfunkel came out to present, he was relieved because both spoke French and actually pronounced the band's name correctly! Making history with that win gave them a kind of validation for the music they were performing, and since then, they have played in every single state more than three times!</p>
  </div>
  <div class="aspect-[3/4] w-full overflow-hidden rounded-xl border border-neutral-200 shadow-sm dark:border-neutral-800">
    <img src="/images/posts/2026-09-18-musical-legacy/musical-legacy-beausoleil-2.webp" alt="BeauSoleil members performing live Cajun reel medley on stage" class="w-full h-full object-cover" />
  </div>
</div>

<div class="mb-8 space-y-3">
  <p>BeauSoleil featured David Doucet on guitar, Chad Huval on accordion/triangle, Bill Bennett on bass, and Mitchell Reed on violin. BeauSoleil performed a song about their grandparents—with a line about how they never uttered a disturbing word, which he admits is a complete lie because they cussed all the time—called "La Terre de Mon Grand-père"; "La Valse De BeauSoleil," about crazy love; a song about what was going on with Zydeco music in the early 1970s called "Zydeco Gris-Gris" in Creole; another tune about the diversity of the music, "La Bête Taille," which translates to "you little wild thing"; and a Cajun reel medley of three songs: two by Dennis McGee and one by Wayne Perry.</p>
  <p class="italic font-medium">"Ah-YEE!"</p>
</div>

  <p>All in all, it was a fantastic experience to witness each of these artists performing. It truly highlighted the importance of Louisiana musicians and their influence across our nation and worldwide.</p>
